0.7.024 October 2026Latest

Promote between clusters, namespace snapshots, and runbooks

Move what works from one cluster to another, share a namespace with people who can’t reach it, and keep your team’s know-how next to the objects.

Promote from one cluster to another

“Promote to another cluster…”, in the menu of a workload, service, config map, ingress and more, or from Compare across clusters, copies an object to another cluster. Change its namespace and the images of its containers first, and bring the config maps its pods use, and their secrets if you choose.

Kube Desk leaves out what a cluster fills in (status, identifiers, owners, cluster IPs, node ports), shows the diff of each object in the target, says what is missing there, such as a secret the pods need, and creates the namespace if it has to. Production targets ask you to type their name; the timeline names the change kubedesk-promote.

Namespace snapshots

Take a snapshot of a namespace, from the overview or ⌘K, into a .kubedesk file: every object, its events, the nodes its pods run on, the last 300 lines of each container’s logs and of its previous run, and its timeline. Secret values never go in the file, and tokens in the logs are masked.

Anyone with Kube Desk opens the file from the Finder, or with Open snapshot on the clusters page, and browses it as a read-only cluster without access to the real one: lists, details, logs, map, incident reports, and the assistant.

Runbooks

Write what to check and do when something fails, in Markdown, for an object, a namespace or a cluster, with wildcards. Links such as [Restart](kubedesk:restart) become buttons for the app’s actions, and run after the usual confirmation. A runbook shows in the Runbook tab of what it is for, and on the overview of its namespace; the Runbooks page lists them all.

They are Markdown files in a folder you choose, such as one in a git repository your team shares. The assistant and MCP agents read them, through a new get_runbooks tool, before proposing a fix.

Also

  • The map shows Gateway API gateways and HTTP routes, where the cluster serves them.
0.6.024 October 2026

Helm releases, a map of every app, and buttons that follow your access

See what Helm deployed and roll it back, see how an app fits together, and know what your credentials allow before they stop working.

Helm releases

A new Helm page lists the releases of a namespace, or of the cluster, with their chart, app version, revision and status. Open one to see its values (the ones given at install, or with the chart’s defaults), what it rendered, its notes, the objects it deployed, and its history.

Compare any two revisions, values or manifests, and roll back to one: Kube Desk shows first what the rollback changes and what Helm deletes, and asks you to type the name of a production cluster. Releases are read from the cluster, so reading them needs no helm command; rolling back runs helm rollback, so that Helm records it.

A map of how things connect

The Map page draws a namespace as its apps: ingresses, the services they route to, the workloads behind them, their pods, and the config maps, secrets and claims those pods use. Each object shows its state, and an app with a problem is outlined; a service with no pods, or an ingress pointing at a service that does not exist, stands out. Point at an object to light its connections, click it to open it. Workloads, services, ingresses and pods have a Map tab of the part they belong to.

Buttons follow your access

Kube Desk asks the cluster what your user may do, as kubectl auth can-i does, and disables what it may not: deleting, scaling, restarting, editing YAML, opening a shell, creating objects. Hover a disabled button to see why.

Kubeconfig health

Kube Desk checks every kubeconfig entry, without connecting: client certificates, CA certificates and tokens that expired or soon will, credential plugins that are not installed (with how to install them), the removed gcp and azure auth providers, servers reached over plain HTTP or without checking their certificate, and clusters added twice. Problems show on the clusters page, in each cluster’s settings, and under Kubeconfig health in ⌘K.

The assistant

  • It can also use Claude in Google Vertex AI, signing in with your gcloud login.
  • Chats are saved: open a past one from the clock in the assistant’s header, and go on with it.
  • It reads Helm releases too, as do MCP agents, through a new helm_releases tool.

Good to know when upgrading

  • Rolling back needs the helm command on your login shell’s PATH; reading releases needs only access to their Secrets.
  • The map shows built-in kinds; Gateway API routes and custom resources are not on it yet.
0.5.023 October 2026

An assistant in the app

Ask about your clusters in your own words, and approve each change it proposes.

Press ⌘J, or Assistant in the title bar, and ask: why a pod fails, what changed in the last hour, how a namespace is doing. The assistant is Claude in Microsoft Foundry, with your Azure resource and API key, which Kube Desk keeps in your macOS keychain.

It sees every cluster in Kube Desk, starting from an overview of nodes, namespaces and their pods, and knows what you have open. It reads resources, logs, events, the timeline and incident reports, searches and compares clusters, and shows each step it takes. When it wants to change something, Kube Desk shows the diff, what the change affects and the assistant’s reason, and makes it only if you approve. Production clusters ask you to type their name, and read-only clusters refuse.

  • A fallback deployment answers requests the main one declines.
  • MCP stays a separate option, for agents outside the app.
  • A cluster_overview tool, for the assistant and MCP agents, summarizes a whole cluster at once.

0.4.022 October 2026

AI agents with approval, search and compare across clusters

Let AI agents work with your clusters safely, and see many clusters as one.

AI agents, with you approving every change

Kube Desk is now an MCP server, so that Claude Code, Cursor, Codex, Claude Desktop and other MCP clients can work with your clusters. Turn it on under AI agents (the robot in the title bar, or ⌘K), copy the command for your client, and choose for each cluster what agents may do: nothing, read, or propose changes.

Agents can list and read resources, read logs and events, see what changed and who changed it, get incident reports, search and compare clusters, and preview what a change would affect. When an agent wants to apply YAML, delete, scale or restart, Kube Desk shows you the change, a diff of each object, what it affects and the agent’s reason, and makes it only if you approve.

Production clusters ask you to type their name, read-only clusters refuse, and changes carry the agent’s name, so the timeline shows who made them. Secret values never reach agents, and tokens and passwords in logs are masked. The server listens on this Mac only, behind a token.

Search every cluster at once

⇧⌘F, or “Search every cluster” in ⌘K, finds objects across all your clusters as you type: by name, image:nginx, app=web, kind:svc, ns:shop, node:…, is:failing or status:CrashLoopBackOff. Results come in cluster by cluster; click one to open it there.

Compare across clusters

“Compare across clusters” lines up a deployment, stateful set, daemon set, service, config map, secret, ingress, cron job or autoscaler in each cluster: images, replicas, resources, environment, ports, volumes, keys. Fields that differ are highlighted, and a YAML diff shows everything between two clusters. Secret values are compared by fingerprint only.

See what Save changes

Saving YAML now shows the diff the cluster will get, and says so when nothing changed.

Good to know when upgrading

  • The MCP server is off by default, and every cluster starts at Off for agents.
  • A change waits 5 minutes for approval; after that the agent gets an error and can ask again.
  • Search covers pods, workloads, services, ingresses, config maps, nodes and namespaces, not custom resources yet.
0.3.022 October 2026

Timeline of changes, impact before you act, incident reports

Three questions during an incident: what just changed, what will this break, and what do I send to whoever is helping?

A timeline of what changed

In the namespaces you watch, Kube Desk records every change while it runs and keeps it for 7 days: deployments, stateful sets, daemon sets, services, ingresses, config maps, secrets, cron jobs and autoscalers, pods restarting or becoming ready, nodes going down, and warning events.

Each change says what changed in plain words (“image api: api:1 → api:2”, “replicas 2 → 3”, “env DATABASE_URL changed”), who changed it (kubectl, Helm, Argo CD, a controller), and shows a diff of before and after. When Kube Desk starts again, it finds what was changed, created or deleted while it was closed. Find it under Changes in the sidebar, in the History tab of each object, and on the overview. Secret values never reach the timeline: only which keys changed.

See what a change breaks, before you make it

Deleting, scaling, restarting and draining now show what else they affect: services left without endpoints, ingress routes that stop working, disruption budgets that would be broken, pods without a controller that would be gone for good, data in emptyDir and volumes that would be lost, config maps and secrets that pods need, pods that would not fit on the other nodes, autoscalers and Helm, Argo CD or Flux that would fight the change. Scaling updates the preview as you type the replicas.

Incident reports in one click

For a pod or a workload, Incident report gathers its state, conditions, events, the last lines of logs of every container and those before its last restart, its node, the recent changes of its namespace and what depends on it, into Markdown to paste into Slack, an issue or an AI assistant. Tokens, keys, passwords and credentials in URLs are masked.

0.2.022 October 2026

Watchlist, workload logs, production guardrails

Watch namespaces, and hear about problems

Watch a namespace or a whole cluster from the title bar, the ⌘K palette or the menu bar. Kube Desk keeps watching in the background, also with its window closed, and sends a notification when a pod crash loops, runs out of memory, fails to pull its image or cannot be scheduled, a rollout gets stuck, or a node goes down. A menu bar icon counts the problems and lists them; click one to open it. When many things break at once, you get a single notification.

Logs of a whole workload

Deployments, stateful sets, daemon sets, replica sets, jobs and services have a Logs tab with the logs of all their pods, interleaved by time and colored by pod. Pods that start during a rollout join in. Hide pods with a click, and save the logs shown to a file.

Production guardrails

Mark a cluster as production: it shows in red, and every change, from deleting to scaling or saving YAML, asks you to type the cluster’s name. Read-only clusters refuse all changes and shells; the app enforces this itself, not just its buttons. Clusters whose names say “prod” offer to be protected.

CPU and memory in lists

Pods and nodes show what they use, from metrics-server, with a bar against their limits or what the node can allocate.

Also

  • Log lines are colored by level, for JSON, logfmt, klog and bracketed levels.
  • The ⌘K palette no longer lists results that do not match.
  • Completed pods no longer look unhealthy, long commands are folded, and badges are no longer cut off.
