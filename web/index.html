Every cluster, one keystroke away.

Kube Desk is a fast Kubernetes desktop client for people who juggle many clusters. Every context in your kubeconfig, in one small app: switch in a keystroke, watch resources change live, tail logs, open a shell, edit YAML.

Download for macOS Source on GitHub

Free and open source (MIT)macOS, Linux, WindowsNo telemetry Other systems

This is a working preview. Press 1 2 3 4 to switch clusters and 0 for all of them. Open a page, switch away and come back: it’s where you left it.

Built around switching between clusters.

Kube Desk takes the parts of Headlamp and Lens you use every day and makes moving between clusters the core of the app, not an afterthought in a dropdown.

One app for every cluster

It reads every context in ~/.kube/config and $KUBECONFIG. Add more by picking a file, pasting a kubeconfig, or entering a server and a token.

Switching is instant

Each cluster keeps its own page, namespace and open object, and its data stays live for a while after you leave. Coming back shows it as you left it.

Small and light

One Go binary, a 14 MB download. It uses the system’s WebView instead of bundling Chromium, and starts like a native app.

Your kubeconfig is never modified

Clusters you add in the app live in its own data folder. Files that gcloud, aws or az rewrite are read in place, so changes show up.

Hear about problems, even with the window closed.

Watch a namespace or a whole cluster. Kube Desk keeps watching in the background and tells you when something goes wrong. When many things break at once, you get one notification, not forty.

  • Crash loops, out of memory, image pull errors, pods that can’t be scheduled
  • Rollouts that get stuck, and nodes that go down
  • A menu bar icon counts the problems; click one to open it

Delete, scale, restart and drain

Know what breaks before you click.

Every risky action shows first what else it affects. Mark a cluster as production and it shows in red, and every change asks you to type its name. Read-only clusters refuse every change and shell, enforced by the app itself, not just by hidden buttons.

  • Services left without endpoints, ingress routes that stop working
  • Disruption budgets, data in volumes, pods that won’t fit elsewhere
  • Objects that Helm, Argo CD or Flux would put back

Sidebar → Map, and the Map tab of every workload

See how an app fits together.

The map draws each app of a namespace from left to right: the ingress, the services it routes to, the workloads behind them, their pods, and the config they use. Every object shows its state, live, and an app with a problem is outlined.

  • A service that selects no pods, or an ingress pointing at a service that’s gone, stands out
  • Point at an object to light up what it connects to; click it to open it
  • Workloads with many pods fold them into one box

⌘J Assistant, in the app

Ask in your own words. Approve every change.

The assistant is Claude, through your own cloud: Microsoft Foundry with your Azure API key, or Google Vertex AI with your gcloud login. It sees every cluster in Kube Desk and what you have open, so “why is this pod crashing?” is enough.

  • It starts from a whole-cluster overview, then reads logs, events, changes, Helm releases and incident reports
  • Every step it takes shows in the chat
  • A change it proposes opens the same approval as agents: the diff, what it affects, and its reason
  • Your API key stays in the system’s keychain; chats are saved on your computer, to go on with later

Private by design.

  • No telemetry. Kube Desk only talks to the API servers in your kubeconfigs and to the update server, and, when you use the assistant, to your own Foundry resource or Vertex AI project.
  • Chats stay on your Mac. The assistant’s conversations are saved in the app’s own folder, and Secret values never reach it.
  • Your files stay yours. It never writes to your kubeconfig. Pasted kubeconfigs and tokens are stored with 0600 permissions in the app’s own folder.
  • Signed updates. Releases are signed with Ed25519, and the app checks the signature before installing.
  • Open source. Read every line on GitHub, under the MIT license.
